The Stitcher

Chapter One: The First Stitch Rozdział Pierwszy: Pierwszy Szew

A Novel by Bartosz Osiej

Open Otwórz ▼

The smell hit him first.

Not the smell of death — Marcus had smelled death before, in Vietnam, in the alleys of Detroit, in the back rooms of funeral homes where nobody bothered with embalming. No. This was different. This was sweet. Cloying. Like vanilla mixed with something rotten underneath, something that crawled into your sinuses and nested there.

Marcus Delgado stood in the doorway of apartment 4B and tried not to breathe.

"Jesus Christ," said Officer Dan Kowalski behind him, his voice thin and reedy, the way voices got when men tried not to vomit. "Jesus fucking Christ."

Marcus didn't respond. He was looking at the room.

It had been a living room once. You could tell by the couch — a floral-print monstrosity from the eighties, the kind your grandmother might have owned, with cat hair ground into the cushions and a permanent dent where someone had sat watching television for thirty years. There was a coffee table with a ring of water stains. A TV mounted on the wall, showing static because nobody had bothered to change the channel. A bookshelf filled with paperbacks whose spines had cracked from being read too many times.

Normal. Ordinary. The kind of room you'd find in any apartment building in any city in America.

Except for the dolls.

They were everywhere.

On the couch. On the coffee table. On the bookshelf. Hanging from the ceiling fan. Standing in the corners like sentinels. Lining the windowsill. Piled in the bathtub, visible through the open bathroom door. There must have been fifty of them. Maybe a hundred.

And they were all different.

Some were small — the size of a baby's fist, with tiny stitched faces and button eyes. Some were medium — the size of a toddler, with articulated limbs and hair made from actual human hair. And some were large — the size of a grown man, standing upright on wooden stands, their faces frozen in expressions that were almost, but not quite, human.

Almost.

That was the worst part. Not the dolls themselves — Marcus had seen worse things in his career, things that would give him nightmares for years to come. No. The worst part was how almost human they looked. The way their skin caught the light. The way their eyes seemed to follow you when you moved. The way their mouths were stitched into smiles that were just a little too wide, a little too knowing, as if they understood something about you that you didn't want to know yourself.

"Detective Delgado?" Kowalski's voice was getting higher. "What do you want to do?"

Marcus finally tore his gaze away from the nearest doll — a medium-sized figure in a Victorian dress, with blonde hair and blue eyes that were actually blue iris, not painted — and looked at the apartment's only other occupant.

She sat in a rocking chair in the corner, the only empty space in the room, surrounded by her creations. She was old — ancient, really, with skin like crumpled paper and white hair pulled back in a bun so tight it stretched her face into a permanent expression of surprise. Her hands were folded in her lap, and Marcus could see that they were steady. Not shaking. Not trembling with age or fear or the adrenaline of being discovered.

Steady.

Like the hands of a surgeon.

"Mrs. Harlow," Marcus said, his voice carefully neutral. "I'm Detective Marcus Delgado, Chicago Police Department. Do you know why we're here?"

The old woman looked at him. Her eyes were sharp — not the cloudy, senile eyes he'd expected, but bright and clear and focused, like a hawk watching a field mouse.

"You're here about the dolls," she said.

It wasn't a question.

"We're here about Martha Henderson," Marcus said. "Your neighbor in 4A. She's been missing for three days. Her daughter reported her missing yesterday. We have reason to believe she may have been—"

"She's in the blue one," Mrs. Harlow said.

Marcus blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The blue doll. On the bookshelf. Third from the left." She nodded toward the bookshelf, and Marcus followed her gaze with a feeling of dread that settled in his stomach like a cold stone. "That's Martha. She was very cooperative. I told her it would be painless, and it was. She barely made a sound."

Kowalski made a sound behind him. It might have been a word. It might have been a gag. Marcus wasn't sure, and he didn't turn to look.

"Mrs. Harlow," he said, his voice still carefully neutral, though his hands were starting to shake. "Are you telling me that you—"

"Killed her?" The old woman tilted her head, considering the question. "I suppose so, in the technical sense. Though I prefer to think of it as preservation. Martha was seventy-three years old. Arthritis in both hands. Bad knees. She couldn't walk without her cane, and even then she shuffled more than walked. She spent her days in that apartment, alone, watching television and eating microwave dinners. She told me once that she wished someone would remember her after she was gone."

Mrs. Harlow reached out and touched the nearest doll — a medium-sized figure in a blue dress, with gray hair and kind eyes and a smile that was almost exactly like the smile Marcus had seen in Martha Henderson's missing persons photo.

"Now she'll be remembered forever," she said. "Her skin is soft. Her hair is still thick. And her face—" She paused, her fingers tracing the doll's stitched cheek. "Her face is exactly as it was. I'm very proud of that one."

Marcus felt the vomit rise in his throat. He swallowed it down, tasting acid and bile.

"You killed her," he said. "You killed Martha Henderson. And you... you turned her into a... into a..."

"A doll," Mrs. Harlow supplied helpfully. "Yes. That's correct."

"Why?"

The old woman looked at him, and for the first time, Marcus saw something flicker in her eyes. Not remorse. Not guilt. Not the wild, unhinged look of a madwoman caught in the act of her madness.

Hunger.

"That's the wrong question, Detective," she said softly. "The right question isn't why. The right question is how."

And then she smiled.

It was a strange smile — not the wide, knowing smile of her dolls, but a small, private smile, the smile of someone who knew a secret that the rest of the world would never understand.

"Would you like to see?" she asked.

The Author

Bartosz Osiej

Bartosz Osiej is a Polish writer who crafts fiction in English — a deliberate act of linguistic displacement that mirrors the themes of his work. His debut series, The Stitcher Trilogy, explores the thin line between creation and obsession, preservation and madness, art and the terrible beauty of being called by something you cannot name. Bartosz Osiej to polski pisarz, który tworzy fikcję w języku angielskim — celowy akt lingwistycznej dezlokacji, który odzwierciedla tematykę jego dzieł. Jego debiutancka seria, Trylogia Szwaczki, eksploruje cienką granicę między tworzeniem a obsesją, zachowaniem a szaleństwem, sztuką a straszliwym pięknem bycia wołanym.

He lives in Poland, writes in English, and believes that the most honest fiction is the kind that makes you unable to look away — even when you want to. Mieszka w Polsce, pisze po angielsku i wierzy, że najuczciwsza fikcja to ta, która nie pozwala oderwać wzroku — nawet gdybyś chciał.
